ADHD Weekly Planner: A Free Printable Template With Three Lines a Day

The FOCO ADHD weekly planner is a one page printable sheet with seven day rows, three lines per day, and one box at the top for the single thing that matters most this week. The three line cap is the mechanism, because a week that holds twenty one items is a week you will stop looking at by Tuesday. Print it, name the one thing, write its first physical action, then fill in the days.

Key Takeaways

  • A weekly view is the only zoom level most ADHD brains stay in. A day is too short to survive one bad morning, a month is too far away to feel real.
  • Three lines per day is a hard cap. The limit is what forces you to choose, which is the part a longer list quietly skips.
  • The sheet asks for one thing for the whole week, on the assumption that some weeks only have room for one.
  • Every plan needs a first physical action written down, because the task is not what you start, the movement is.
  • Blank days carry no penalty. Nothing on the sheet records what you missed, so there is no streak to break and no reason to stop opening it.

What is on the ADHD weekly planner?

One page, seven day rows, and one box that sits above all of them.

The One Thing This Week is the top band. One wide line for the thing that matters, and underneath it a second line labelled first physical action.

Seven day rows run down the page. Monday to Friday get three lines each, Saturday and Sunday get two, because pretending the weekend has the same capacity is how weekends get eaten.

Each line has a checkbox. The first box in every day is solid purple, the other two are faint, which is a quiet signal about which one actually needs to happen.

Across the top there is a week of field and an energy check with three options. Naming the week low before you plan it is more useful than discovering it on Thursday.

Why does a weekly view work better than a daily one?

Because it is the only zoom level that survives a bad day.

Plan a single day and one difficult morning wipes the whole page, so you stop opening it. Plan a month and nothing on it feels close enough to start, so nothing gets touched until the deadline arrives.

A week is long enough that Tuesday can go badly without the plan dying, and short enough that Friday still feels real.

This is partly a time perception issue. Time blindness flattens the difference between three weeks away and tomorrow, and a weekly page is short enough that the horizon stays inside the range you can actually feel.

What does the one thing this week mean?

It means if nothing else happens, that is the thing you would want to have done.

Not the most urgent. Not the one someone else is chasing you about. The one that changes something.

Most weekly planners assume the week is a container to be filled. This one assumes it is a week where you might only get one real thing done, and asks you to decide in advance which one that is.

Writing it at the top also protects it. When Wednesday goes wrong and you are triaging, the box is still there telling you what the week was supposed to be for.

If naming one thing feels impossible because everything is equally loud, that is a task breakdown problem rather than a planning one. The 1-3-5 rule is a reasonable way to sort a pile before it reaches this page.

Why does the sheet ask for a first physical action?

Because a task is not what you start. A movement is.

"Do the invoices" does not start. "Open the invoice folder" starts, and it starts because it is too small for your brain to build resistance against.

This is the single most useful field on the page and the one people skip. The gap between deciding to do something and doing it is where the week goes, and a written first action shortens it.

The distinction matters because task paralysis is not a knowledge failure. You already know what needs doing. The stall happens at the physical initiation step, so that is the step the page should hold.

Keep it small enough to feel almost silly. If the first action takes more than two minutes, it is not the first action.

What do you do when the week collapses on Wednesday?

Move things, or admit they were never really tasks.

The footer on the sheet says blank days are not failures. That is not encouragement, it is an instruction about how to use the page.

Something that has been pushed from Monday to Wednesday to Friday for three weeks is not a task you keep failing to do. It is a task you have decided against without saying so, and writing it a fourth time only adds weight.

When the week is genuinely gone, stop working from the weekly sheet and switch to a single day at a time with the ADHD daily planner. Different sheet, different question.

Which format do you actually need?

Start weekly. Add the others only if you notice something missing.

The weekly sheet is the default because it is the planning horizon. The daily sheet is triage for days that have already gone wrong. A monthly view is for seeing fixed points, appointments and deadlines, not for holding tasks.

Format matters less than most planner advice suggests. What matters is whether the page has a cap, whether it names a first action, and whether it punishes you for gaps. Those three properties predict whether a system survives more than the layout does.

Where paper runs out

The sheet can hold the plan. It cannot make the first move with you.

Paper is passive by design, which is its advantage and its ceiling. It will sit there being correct while nothing happens.

FOCO shows one small step at a time instead of the whole task, and its AI focus companion stays present while you work rather than handing you a list and leaving. The printable and the app cover different halves of the same problem, which is why the page has both.

FAQ

Can I print the weekly planner on A4?

Yes. Set the printer to fit or scale to page, since the sheet uses proportional blocks rather than fixed positions, so nothing gets cut off.

Why do Saturday and Sunday have fewer lines?

Because weekends have less structure and usually less capacity, and giving them the same three slots quietly turns rest days into overflow for the week you did not finish.

Should I plan the week on Sunday or Monday?

Whichever you will actually do. Sunday gives you a calmer decision, Monday gives you accurate information about your energy, and a sheet filled in on Monday morning beats a perfect Sunday ritual you skip.

What happens if I only fill in the top box and nothing else?

That is a legitimate use of the page. Naming one thing and its first action is most of the value, and the seven rows are there for the weeks you have room for more.

The Bottom Line

The ADHD weekly planner is one page with a hard cap of three lines a day and one box for the thing that matters most. The cap is not a design constraint, it is the working part.

Print two or three at once and put them somewhere you already look. A sheet in a folder is a sheet you have already lost.
