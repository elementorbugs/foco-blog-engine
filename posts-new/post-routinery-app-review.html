Short answer: Routinery is a strong ADHD-friendly routine app: you split a routine into 1-5 minute steps, and each step runs on its own timer while a voice tells you what comes next. It is best for repeating sequences like mornings, bedtime and medication, and its free plan covers 2 routines with ads.

  • Try Routinery if your mornings or evenings fall apart between steps, and a voice and timer would keep you moving.
  • Skip it if most of your stuck tasks are one-offs that change every day, or you want calendar sync.
  • Pick FOCO if the hard task is new each time, and you want AI to split it into timed steps for you, with a focus companion on screen.

What does Routinery look like in use?

The screenshots below are Routinery's official App Store screenshots, not our own. We did not test Routinery hands-on, so we describe what the listing shows and what Routinery says the app does.

You build a routine once as an ordered list of small steps, each with its own length: make bed 5 minutes, drink water 1 minute, stretch 10 minutes. Steps can be skipped, duplicated or reordered.

Then you press play. Each step gets a countdown, a voice reads it aloud, and the next step is already shown at the bottom, so the routine keeps running while you shower or make coffee.

Around the routines sit a day planner with Morning, Afternoon and Evening sections, an Anytime list for tasks you're not ready for, streaks shown as a growing plant, and the option to share a routine's progress with a friend or family member.

Is Routinery built for ADHD?

Partly. It is a general routine app, but its App Store listing names "adults with ADHD who know the task and still can't start" first among its users and says Forbes Health named it a best ADHD app of 2025.

Its design choices fit ADHD well: small steps, nothing to decide mid-routine, and a voice that pulls you out of a step before an afternoon disappears. It also states plainly that it is not a medical device and does not diagnose or treat ADHD.

What does Routinery get right for ADHD brains?

It takes decisions out of the moment. You set the order once, on a calm day, so a groggy morning only asks you to press play; that eases the load on executive function, the brain skills behind planning, starting and switching tasks (our executive function guide explains more).

It makes steps small enough to start. "Study" is hard to begin, but "sit at the desk, 1 minute" is not, which matches how small a first step should be when you're stuck.

It puts time outside your head. Each step's countdown, plus the voice cue at the end, works as an external clock for time blindness (see our time blindness guide), and it softens the jump from one activity to the next, which we cover in ADHD transitions.

For a concrete starting point, our ADHD morning routine guide lays out the kind of sequence Routinery is built to run.

Where does Routinery fall short?

It is built for repetition. Routines shine when the same steps happen in the same order, but a one-off task like "write the grant report" still needs you to break it into steps yourself, either by hand or through an AI assistant connected over MCP.

That matters because the hardest stuck tasks are often new or vague. Knowing that a step exists is one problem; facing an unclear task is the core of task initiation trouble, explained in our guide to task initiation deficit.

The free plan is small: 2 routines, with ads. Routinery's listing also says there is no direct calendar or Apple Health sync and no web version.

How much does Routinery cost?

Routinery is free for 2 routines, and Premium removes the cap and the ads. Premium comes as monthly, 6-month or yearly plans, and the US App Store lists prices including $5 a month, $24 for 6 months and $36 a year, plus a Family plan.

The App Store shows several other price points as well, and Routinery's site advertises a Lifetime plan at a discount (Routinery's site). Check the paywall in the app for your current offer.

How is Routinery different from FOCO?

Both apps time small steps, but they start from different places. Routinery runs routines you set up in advance; FOCO, the app we make, starts from the single task you're stuck on today.

In FOCO you capture the task by typing, speaking or photographing a list, then one tap and AI splits it into small steps with minutes for each. Step one opens into a focus session with calm music and an AI companion on screen.

Many people would use both: Routinery for the morning and bedtime that repeat, FOCO for the task that shows up once and won't budge.

Who should try Routinery?

Try Routinery if:

  • Your routines stall between steps, not at the start.
  • A voice and a countdown help you more than a checklist.
  • You want a free plan to test it, and you're on iPhone or Android.

Look elsewhere if:

  • You need tasks synced with your calendar; see TickTick vs Structured.

FAQ

Is Routinery good for ADHD?

It can be, especially for repeating routines like mornings, bedtime and medication. Small timed steps and voice cues suit many ADHD brains, though it is not a medical treatment.

Is Routinery free?

Yes, for up to 2 routines, with ads and no account needed. Premium removes the routine cap and the ads.

How much is Routinery Premium?

Premium is sold monthly, for 6 months or yearly; the US App Store lists $5 a month and $36 a year among its prices. Check the in-app paywall for your current offer.

Is Routinery on Android?

Yes. Routinery is on Google Play as well as the App Store, and also runs on iPad, Mac and Apple Watch, but there is no web version.

Does Routinery sync with Google Calendar?

Not directly. Its listing says there is no direct calendar sync, though an AI assistant connected over MCP can work with both Routinery and your calendar.

What is the difference between Routinery and FOCO?

Routinery runs routines you set up in advance, step by step. FOCO uses AI to split a single stuck task into timed steps and keeps a focus companion on screen while you do them.
