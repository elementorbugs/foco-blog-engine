The Wall of Awful: Why Starting Feels Impossible With ADHD

The Wall of Awful is a metaphor from ADHD coach Brendan Mahan for the emotional barrier that builds up in front of tasks you have failed at before. Every missed deadline, disappointed person and quiet guilt adds a brick, so the wall stands between you and starting even when the task itself is small. You cannot think your way through it, but you can lower it, which is what FOCO's one-step-at-a-time approach and its AI body-doubling companion are built to do.

Key Takeaways

  • The Wall of Awful is not the task. It is the accumulated emotion attached to the task, which is why a two-minute errand can feel as heavy as a project.
  • The bricks are made of past failure, other people's disappointment, fear of being rejected again, and guilt that hardens into shame.
  • The wall is tallest at the exact moment of starting, which is why the delay happens before any work begins rather than during it.
  • Anger gets you through the wall but the cost lands on the people around you, so it is the most expensive option that still technically works.
  • The wall gets lower when the emotion is acknowledged and the first step is small enough that it does not need a decision.

What is the Wall of Awful?

The Wall of Awful is the emotional barrier that builds up in front of a task you have struggled with before, made of the feelings that accumulate every time it goes badly.

The metaphor comes from Brendan Mahan, an ADHD coach and host of the ADHD Essentials podcast, who developed it to explain something clients kept describing. They were not confused about what to do. They were standing in front of it, unable to move.

The important part of the metaphor is that the wall is real, and it is not made of laziness. It is made of history.

A task you have never attempted has no wall. A task you have failed at eleven times has a tall one, and each attempt that ends badly makes the twelfth harder rather than easier.

This is the piece that most advice gets backwards. It treats the task as the obstacle and prescribes better planning, when the obstacle is what you feel about the task before you touch it.

What are the bricks actually made of?

Not one feeling. Mahan describes a single failure laying several bricks at once.

There is the failure itself. Then the disappointment of whoever was counting on you. Then the fear that it will happen again and cost you the relationship. Then guilt, which over enough repetitions stops being about the thing you did and becomes about who you are.

That last shift is the one that does the damage. Mahan describes shame as the feeling of being the mistake rather than having made one.

Adults with ADHD describe this layer directly and without prompting. One comment in an ADHD community named it as a permanent resident.

"the 'I hate myself, I can't believe I still haven't done that' section of my brain"

Another, in a thread with thousands of responses, described a lifetime of not being able to explain it.

"she thought she was crazy, or lazy, or stupid, that she was never able to adequately explain why she wouldn't be able to move or start a task"

There is research behind why this emotional layer sits so close to the surface. A 2023 systematic review in PLoS One argues that emotion dysregulation should be treated as a core symptom of adult ADHD rather than a side effect, and cites earlier estimates that it affects roughly 34 to 70 percent of adults with the diagnosis.

If emotional regulation is part of the condition rather than a personal failing, then a wall built out of unprocessed emotion is not a character problem. It is the predictable result.

Why is the wall highest right before you start?

Because starting is the moment the feeling arrives, and nothing about the task has happened yet to counter it.

This is why the delay is almost never in the middle of the work. Once people are in, they are frequently fine, sometimes for hours. The blockage sits at the threshold.

One widely shared post put the whole thing in a single line with no body text at all, and collected thousands of upvotes for it.

"Task Initiation is more laborious than the task itself"

That is the wall described from the inside. The effort is front-loaded onto the part where nothing is being produced, which makes it invisible to everyone else.

Mechanically this overlaps with what we cover in task initiation deficit, where the gap sits between intention and the first physical action. The Wall of Awful adds the emotional reason that gap is so hard to cross on a task with history behind it.

It also explains a pattern that looks irrational from outside. Someone will reorganise an entire cupboard rather than open one envelope, because the cupboard has no wall in front of it and the envelope has a tall one.

What are the ways people deal with the wall?

Mahan's model describes four common responses, only one of which is both effective and free of collateral damage.

Four responses to the wall

Same wall, same task. What changes is the strategy and what it costs you.

1

Stare at it

You face the task, fully aware of it, and nothing moves. Hours pass at the threshold.

Does not work
2

Go around it

You find something else urgent. The wall is effectively infinitely wide, so the detour never reaches the other side.

Does not work
3

Smash through it

Anger generates enough force to start. The task gets done and the people nearby absorb the blast.

Works, costs relationships
4

Climb it

You acknowledge the feeling instead of arguing with it, and move in small deliberate steps until you are over.

Works

Most adults cycle through 1 and 2 for days, reach 3 the night before it is due, and conclude they only work under pressure.

Seen laid out like this, one thing becomes obvious. Three of the four options are things you are probably already doing.

Mahan's forthcoming book adds a fifth move, putting a door in the wall, which is about making the entry point small enough that the wall stops being the thing you have to get over.

Why does smashing through it cost you later?

Because the fuel is anger, and anger has to land somewhere.

This is the option almost nobody names out loud, and it is extremely common. You get furious at the situation, at the person who asked, at yourself, and the fury produces enough activation to start.

It works. That is the problem, because a strategy that works gets repeated.

The bill arrives afterwards. The people closest to you learn that tasks come with a mood, and their reaction adds fresh bricks to the wall for next time, which is the mechanism behind rejection sensitivity compounding over years.

It also teaches you the wrong lesson about yourself. Not "I needed a way in" but "I only function when I am angry or panicking", which is a miserable operating manual to be handed.

Why does the wall feel lower when someone else is there?

Because a second presence changes the emotional state around the task without changing the task.

People report this as something that happens to them rather than something they accomplish. One comment reduced it to four words.

"her presence unlocks my paralysis"

Note what is not happening there. Nobody is helping with the work, giving advice, or checking up. The wall is simply shorter with someone in the room.

This is why body doubling works on a problem that better scheduling does not touch. It is aimed at the emotional layer, which is where the blockage actually is.

It is also the same reason a rule you set for yourself slides off. The wall is emotional, and a rule you can quietly cancel adds no emotional weight on the other side to pull you over.

How do you climb it instead?

You name the feeling, then make the first move small enough that it does not require you to feel differently first.

Climbing is slower than smashing and it is genuinely less satisfying, which is why it gets skipped. What follows is the version that adults describe as holding, rather than the sticker charts most articles on this topic reach for.

Say what the feeling is. Out loud or on paper, in plain words. Dread, embarrassment about how late it is, fear of what you will find when you open the file. Naming it takes the emotion out of the background where it operates unchallenged.

Aim at the doorway, not the room. Not "do the taxes" but "open the folder". The wall guards the whole task, and it is much lower in front of one concrete action, which is what task breakdown is for.

Do not wait to feel ready. Readiness is on the other side of the wall, not this side. The order is action first, feeling second, which is the reverse of how it seems it should work.

Borrow a presence for the first few minutes. The hardest stretch is the threshold, and that is precisely where an outside presence lowers the wall most.

Expect the wall to still be there tomorrow. Climbing it once does not demolish it. It does add a brick to the other pile, the one made of times it went fine.

FAQ

Is the Wall of Awful a clinical term?

No. It is a metaphor created by ADHD coach Brendan Mahan, not a diagnosis or a term from the DSM-5. What it describes maps onto documented features of ADHD, including emotional dysregulation and difficulty with task initiation, but the wall itself is a teaching model rather than a clinical construct.

Why do small tasks have the biggest walls?

Because wall height tracks emotional history, not task size. A five-minute phone call you have postponed for three months carries three months of guilt, while a genuinely large task you feel neutral about has almost nothing in front of it.

Is this the same thing as procrastination?

Not quite. Procrastination usually describes choosing something more pleasant instead, whereas this is being unable to move toward the task at all. The difference between procrastination and paralysis is worth knowing, because the two respond to different approaches.

Does the wall ever go away?

Individual walls shrink as you accumulate experiences of the task going fine, but the mechanism does not disappear. Most adults get better at recognising a wall and climbing it early rather than reaching a point where nothing has one.

The Bottom Line

You are not stuck because the task is hard. You are stuck because there is a wall in front of it built from every previous attempt, and no amount of planning removes a wall made of feelings.

What lowers it is naming what you feel, shrinking the first step until it is almost too small to refuse, and not being alone at the threshold. That is a slower route than getting angry enough to smash through, and it is the only one that does not send you the bill later.
