How Small Should ADHD Task Steps Be?

A task step is small enough when you can picture doing it in under two minutes without making any other decision first. If a "step" still requires choosing between options, involves more than one physical action, or makes you pause to think, it's still a task wearing a step's name tag. Break it down one more level.

Key Takeaways

  • The right step size isn't about a fixed number of minutes, it's about removing every decision except "do this one thing."
  • A step that still requires choosing between options is a mini-task, not a step.
  • Steps can also be too small - endless micro-steps create their own kind of overwhelm.

  • The test is behavioral, not theoretical: if you're still stuck after shrinking a step, it's not small enough yet.

Why does step size matter so much for ADHD task initiation?

Advice to "break it into smaller steps" is everywhere, but most of it stops short of saying how small.

For task initiation deficit, the gap isn't between "big task" and "small task." It's between a step that requires a decision and one that doesn't. A step small enough to skip the decision is a step your brain can actually act on without a runway.

"Write the report" still requires deciding what to write first. "Open the document" requires nothing. That difference, not the word count of the step, is what determines whether it gets started.

Research published in JAMA (Volkow et al., 2009) points to differences in dopamine reward circuitry in ADHD brains, part of why a distant, effortful goal generates less motivational pull than an immediate, concrete action does.

What's a simple test for whether a step is small enough?

Ask one question: does doing this step require choosing anything else first?

If the answer is no, and it takes under roughly two minutes, it's sized correctly. If the answer is yes, it's still a task pretending to be a step, and it needs one more round of breaking down.

"Answer emails""Open the inbox tab"

What are the signs a step is still too big?

Three reliable signs a step needs to be broken down further:

It contains the word "and." "Open the laptop and start the file" is two steps joined into one. Split at the "and."

You still feel resistance looking at it. Resistance to a step, not the whole task, means the step is hiding a decision inside it.

You paused before starting to figure out how. A correctly sized step never requires figuring anything out. It only requires doing.

Can a step be too small?

Yes. Splitting "open the document" into "move the mouse to the icon" and "click the icon" adds friction instead of removing it.

Over-fragmenting turns one clear action into a checklist to manage, which becomes its own small executive-function tax. The goal is the smallest step that still feels like a complete, satisfying action, not the smallest step that's technically possible.

How do you break a task down without overthinking the process?

Say the task out loud, then say the very first physical motion it requires, out loud, in one sentence. Not the goal, not the outcome, just the motion.

If that motion still contains a decision, say the motion before that one. Most tasks resolve to a startable step within two or three rounds of this.

How is this different from a general 5-minute rule?

A 5-minute rule sets a time box for how long you commit to working, which helps with continuing a task once started.

Step sizing solves an earlier problem: getting started at all. The two work together - a properly sized first step gets you moving, and a short time commitment keeps you moving once you are.

FAQ: Sizing ADHD Task Steps

Is there an ideal number of minutes for a task step?

Roughly two minutes is a useful rule of thumb, but the real test is whether the step requires a decision. A ten-second step that still requires deciding something is worse than a ninety-second step that doesn't.

What if I can't figure out the first physical motion?

That's usually a sign the task itself is still vague. Naming the very first object you'd need to touch (a specific document, a specific drawer) often reveals the motion.

Should every task on my list be broken into tiny steps?

No. This is most useful for tasks you're already stuck on. Tasks you start easily don't need the extra step.

Why does a small step work when a to-do list item doesn't?

A typical to-do list item still contains a hidden decision about how to begin. A properly sized step has already made that decision for you.

Does this help with tasks that have no clear physical first action, like "think about my career"?

Yes, though the first step becomes something concrete like "open a blank note and write one sentence," which converts an abstract task into a physical one.
