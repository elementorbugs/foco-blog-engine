Why You Can't Follow Your Own Rules With ADHD

You can't follow your own rules because you know they are not real. A rule you set for yourself carries no consequence, and ADHD brains respond far more reliably to what is present and external than to what is internal and delayed. That is why an emailed deadline works and a promise to yourself does not. The fix is not more willpower, it is borrowing structure from outside your own head, which is what FOCO's AI body-doubling companion is built to do.

Key Takeaways

  • Breaking your own rules is not a character flaw. It is what happens when the only enforcer is the same person who wrote the rule.
  • Barkley's model of ADHD describes it as a disorder of self-regulation, where behavior guided by internal rules is affected more than behavior guided by immediate, external consequences.
  • Self-imposed deadlines and self-given rewards fail for the same reason: you can revoke them at any moment, and your brain knows it.
  • External accountability works because it moves the consequence outside you, where you cannot quietly cancel it.
  • What tends to help is an outside presence at the moment of starting, one step at a time, rather than a stricter rule you will break again.

Why can't I follow rules I set for myself?

Because you are both the person making the rule and the person who can cancel it, and your brain treats that rule as optional from the start.

This is one of the most consistently described experiences among adults with ADHD. One post in an ADHD community put it in a single line that collected over a thousand upvotes.

"I can't follow my own rules because I know the girl who made them and she's full of it."

That is the whole mechanism in one sentence. The rule has no authority because its author has no authority over you.

A deadline your manager sets exists whether or not you agree with it. A deadline you set exists only for as long as you keep agreeing to it, and agreement is exactly the thing that runs out.

What is actually happening when you break your own deadline?

Nothing dramatic. The rule simply loses to whatever is happening right now.

Psychologist Russell Barkley's model describes ADHD as a disorder of self-regulation, in which behavior guided by internal rules and future consequences is affected more than behavior guided by immediate ones. Under that model, the problem is not that you do not know the rule. It is the gap between knowing and doing, rooted in executive function.

A self-imposed deadline is an internal rule pointing at a future consequence, which makes it the exact category ADHD makes hardest to act on.

Meanwhile the alternative is right in front of you and available immediately. That contest is not close.

This is also why the feeling is so specific. It is not "I forgot." It is watching yourself decide not to, in real time, and being unable to argue back.

Why doesn't tricking myself work?

Because you are always in on the trick.

Setting your clocks ten minutes fast, telling yourself an event starts earlier than it does, promising yourself a break after thirty minutes of work.

Each one requires you to forget the terms you just wrote. You do not forget them.

Another widely shared comment in an ADHD community described this precisely.

"I can't 'trick' myself. I can't give myself rewards. If I want to do or buy something, I know that I can do whatever I want. I can't give myself fake deadlines because I know they aren't real."

And the version that gets to the reward problem specifically: "If I say, okay after you do 30 minutes of work you can sit down for 15 minutes, I'm just like how about I sit down now."

A reward you control is not a reward. It is a thing you already own, with an optional delay attached.

There is also a compounding version of this. Every trick you try and break teaches you that your own commitments do not hold, which makes the next one weaker than the last.

Where the consequence lives

The same task, the same person. The only variable is who holds the consequence.

Rule you set yourself

  • You can revoke it silently, at any moment
  • Nobody notices if it lapses
  • The consequence is a feeling, and it is delayed
  • Breaking it costs nothing today

Rule held outside you

  • Cancelling it means telling someone
  • Somebody is expecting the thing
  • The consequence is concrete and near
  • Breaking it costs something now

This is why the same person can miss every self-imposed deadline for a month and still deliver on a client call scheduled for Tuesday.

Why do external deadlines work when my own don't?

Because an external deadline is not negotiable by you alone, so the escape hatch closes.

Adults with ADHD describe this contrast constantly. The same person who cannot start a personal project all month will deliver flawlessly when somebody else is waiting.

"It's also the issue I have with setting my own deadlines at work. I know I set them so I know I can change them. Externally set deadlines though? Turns me into a hyperfocused beast."

Notice what changes and what does not. The task is identical. Your skills are identical. The only difference is that the consequence moved outside your control.

This is the same reason a tidy house appears three hours before guests arrive. Not because you found motivation, but because the deadline stopped being yours to move.

Does this mean I just need more discipline?

No, and that framing is what keeps the loop going.

More discipline means building a stricter rule, enforced by the same person who could not enforce the last one. It is the same design with higher stakes, which mostly adds shame when it fails again.

One description of this escalation is worth reading twice: "For every parent I create that can loom over the baby, there's a larger baby created that just brushes the parent aside."

Each new layer of self-imposed authority creates a new self that ignores it. You cannot out-supervise yourself with more of yourself.

The National Institute of Mental Health describes ADHD as an ongoing pattern of inattention, hyperactivity, or impulsivity that interferes with functioning, not a matter of effort. Treating a regulation difficulty as a motivation problem misdiagnoses the thing you are trying to fix.

What actually works instead of self-imposed rules?

Move the structure out of your head and into the room, and make the next action small enough that starting does not need a rule at all.

Three shifts show up repeatedly in what adults with ADHD say actually holds.

Borrow a witness. Working alongside someone, even silently, changes the internal state around a task. This is body doubling, and it works because the accountability is no longer something you have to generate.

Shrink the unit. A rule is only needed when the task is big enough to argue about. One concrete next step, not a plan for the day, removes most of the negotiation. That is the core of task breakdown, and it targets task initiation deficit rather than scheduling.

Externalize the cue. Visible, physical, unavoidable signals do the remembering for you, instead of a commitment you have to keep renewing. The same logic explains why low-maintenance tools survive and feature-heavy ones do not.

FAQ

Is it normal to constantly break promises I make to myself?

Yes, it is one of the most commonly described adult ADHD experiences. A self-imposed rule has no external consequence, and ADHD affects behavior guided by internal rules and delayed outcomes more than behavior guided by immediate ones.

Why can I meet deadlines for other people but not for myself?

Because someone else's deadline cannot be quietly moved. The consequence sits outside you, which makes it concrete and near rather than internal and postponable.

Do reward systems work for adults with ADHD?

Self-administered rewards often do not, because you can take the reward at any time without doing the task. Rewards tend to work better when the timing or the access genuinely sits outside your control.

How do I hold myself accountable if I can't trust my own rules?

Stop trying to hold yourself accountable alone. Put a person, a scheduled session, or a visible external cue between you and the task, and make the first step small enough that it does not require a decision.

The Bottom Line

You are not failing to follow your own rules because you are weak. You are failing because a rule enforced only by its author is not really a rule, and your brain worked that out a long time ago.

The way out is not a stricter promise. It is putting the structure somewhere you cannot quietly cancel it, and shrinking the first step until beginning does not need permission from a version of you who might say no.
