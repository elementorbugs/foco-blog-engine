How free is "free" for each app?

Free comes in four kinds, and the difference matters when a limit hits on a busy day.

  • Free for good: Goblin Tools on the web, Habitica and Finch's core work without ever paying.
  • Generous free plan: Structured, TickTick and Todoist cover most daily use; paying adds extras.
  • Limited free plan: Routinery (2 routines, ads), Focusmate (3 sessions a week), Saner.AI (30 AI messages a month) and Ellie (no time blocking) let you try the core, then cap it.
  • One-time purchase: Goblin Tools' phone apps cost $1.99 once in the US App Store, with no subscription needed.

The 12 best free ADHD apps, by need

1. Goblin Tools, best free app for breaking down tasks

Goblin Tools' Magic ToDo turns one overwhelming task into a list of small steps, with a "spiciness" control for how tiny they get. Its makers describe a free website with no account and nine small single-purpose tools.

The catch: it has no timer, calendar or reminders, so it helps you plan a task, not stay with it. The phone apps are a $1.99 one-time purchase; see our Goblin Tools alternatives for more.

2. Structured, best free visual day planner

Structured shows one day as a single timeline of colored blocks, each sized to how long it takes. Its help center says you can use "almost every feature of Structured for free" (What is Structured Pro?).

The catch: recurring tasks and AI planning are Pro. See how it compares in TickTick vs Structured.

3. TickTick, best free all-in-one app

TickTick puts tasks, a calendar, a habit tracker and a Pomodoro timer in one app. The free plan covers 9 lists with up to 99 tasks each and 5 habits (TickTick Premium).

The catch: the richer calendar views and Timeline are Premium, and all those features can turn setup into a hobby.

4. Todoist, best free app for capturing tasks

Todoist reads plain sentences like "call the dentist tomorrow at 9" and turns them into tasks with dates. The free plan includes 5 projects, reminders and limited Ramble voice sessions.

The catch: no focus timer, and the calendar layout is Pro. Our Todoist vs TickTick comparison helps you pick between the two free plans.

5. Routinery, best free app for routines

Routinery splits a routine into 1-5 minute steps, and each step runs on its own timer while a voice says what comes next. The free plan covers 2 routines with no account needed.

The catch: the free plan shows ads, and more than 2 routines needs Premium. Full details in our Routinery review.

6. Finch, best free self-care app

Finch turns small goals like drinking water or getting out of bed into energy for a virtual pet bird. Missing a goal costs nothing, which avoids the guilt spiral of streak-based apps.

The catch: it doesn't help with work tasks or focus. More in our Finch app review.

7. Habitica, best free gamified habit app

Habitica turns tasks and habits into a role-playing game with quests and a party. Its App Store listing says it "can be fully enjoyed for free," and its subscription is optional.

The catch: missed Dailies cost health points, which can add shame on rough weeks. See our Habitica alternatives for gentler options.

8. Forest, best free app for staying off your phone

Forest grows a virtual tree while you focus and kills it if you leave the app. The core timer is free on iPhone, iPad and Android.

The catch: the Android version shows ads. Read Forest for ADHD for when it helps and when it doesn't.

9. Focus Friend, best free focus timer with a companion

Focus Friend, made with Hank Green, gives you a little bean who gets sad if you quit a focus session early and earns decorations when you finish. It includes break timers and app blocking during sessions, according to its App Store listing.

The catch: extra beans and decorations are paid, and Pro is $3.99 a month, $19.99 a year or $39.99 lifetime in the US App Store.

10. Focusmate, best free body doubling

Focusmate pairs you with another person on video for a focused work session. Body doubling, working with someone else present, helps many people with ADHD start and stay on task (our body doubling guide explains why).

The catch: the free plan allows 3 sessions a week; Plus is $8 a month billed yearly or $12 monthly (Focusmate pricing).

11. Ellie, best free brain-dump planner

Ellie lets you empty your head into a Brain Dump, then pick only what fits today. The free plan includes unlimited tasks and Brain Dump on iPhone, iPad and the web.

The catch: time blocking, calendar sync and recurring tasks are Pro, and there is no Android app. Full details in our Ellie Planner review.

12. Saner.AI, best free app for scattered information

Saner.AI pulls notes, Gmail, Slack and your calendar into one place, with an assistant that finds and plans from them. Its free plan includes 30 AI messages a month and 100 notes.

The catch: 30 messages run out fast, and its mobile apps are rated far lower than its Chrome extension. See our Saner.AI review.

How do you build a free ADHD app setup?

Pick one app for capture and at most one for doing; two free apps you open daily beat five you forget. Here are three setups that cost nothing.

  • For a scattered workday: Todoist to capture everything, plus Goblin Tools when a task feels too big.
  • For time blindness: Structured for the day's shape, plus Focus Friend or Forest to stay with each block.
  • For routines and self-care: Routinery for the morning, plus Finch for the small things on low days.

Keep the setup small on purpose. Our article on why you keep abandoning ADHD planner apps covers the traps that end most attempts within a week.

What are the catches with free ADHD apps?

Free plans usually cap the exact feature you lean on most. Check four things before you commit:

  • Limits that hit at the worst time: a third routine, a sixth project or the 31st AI message.
  • Ads: Routinery's free plan and Forest on Android show them, and an ad is one more chance to drift.
  • Upgrade prompts: paywalls that appear mid-task can pull you off course.
  • Trials that turn into charges: if you start a paid trial, set a reminder two days before it ends.

When is a paid ADHD app worth it?

When you have used a free app for a few weeks and can name the one thing it doesn't do. If that gap is starting the task itself, a free list won't close it.

That gap is task initiation, the space between knowing what to do and doing it, explained in our guide to task initiation deficit.

FAQ

What is the best free ADHD app?

It depends on your main struggle: Goblin Tools for breaking down tasks, Structured for a visual day, TickTick for an all-in-one list. All three are useful without paying.

Is there a completely free ADHD app?

Yes. Goblin Tools is free on the web with no account, Habitica can be fully enjoyed for free, and Finch's core is free.

What is the best free ADHD app for adults?

For most adults, TickTick or Todoist for tasks plus Goblin Tools for breaking down hard ones covers the basics at no cost.

What is the best free app for ADHD time blindness?

Structured, because it shows your day as blocks sized to real time, and almost every feature is free. Pair it with a focus timer like Forest or Focus Friend.

Are free ADHD apps as good as paid ones?

Often, for planning and habits. Paid apps tend to add AI help, higher limits and support for specific problems like starting tasks or coaching.

Is FOCO free?

No. FOCO is $59 a year with a 7-day free trial, or $9 a month; FOCO's browser tools at tryfoco.com/adhd-tools are free with no sign-up.

Can a free app replace ADHD treatment?

No. Apps can support daily structure, but diagnosis and treatment come from a clinician; see NIMH's ADHD overview for options.
