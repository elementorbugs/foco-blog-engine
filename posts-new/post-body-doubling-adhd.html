What is body doubling?

Body doubling is exactly what it sounds like: you work on your task while another person is present and working on theirs. They don't have to help you.

They don't even have to be doing the same thing. Their job is just to be there.

It can happen in person - a friend at the kitchen table, a coworker in the same room, a family member on the couch. It can also happen virtually - a video call where two people work in silence with cameras on, a coworking app, an ADHD focus session.

The term comes from the ADHD coaching world, but the practice is much older. Anyone who's ever found themselves more productive at a coffee shop than alone at home has experienced body doubling without naming it.

For people with ADHD, this isn't a preference. It's often the difference between starting and not starting at all.

Why does body doubling work for ADHD?

The short answer: your brain borrows another person's regulation when it can't generate its own.

The longer answer comes down to three mechanisms.

External accountability without pressure. Knowing someone can see you working triggers a mild, low-stakes form of accountability. It's not as intense as a deadline (which floods you with stress), but it's strong enough to keep you in your seat.

Your brain treats the other person's gaze as a soft anchor.

Co-regulation: one possible explanation. One model for this is co-regulation, the idea that people attune to each other physiologically. It is a plausible explanation rather than a settled one, and the research on body doubling specifically is thin. What is consistent is what people report: in FOCO's survey of 194 adults with ADHD, 87% rated working alongside someone as helpful or life-changing.

For an ADHD brain that struggles with self-regulation, this borrowed regulation is enormous.

Reduced decision load. Working alone, you have to decide what to do, when to start, when to take a break, when to come back. Working alongside someone, those decisions get smaller - you start when they start, you focus when they focus.

The cognitive cost of executive function gets shared.

According to CHADD, executive function struggles are common among people with ADHD, though the degree varies from person to person. Body doubling addresses all three of the executive functions that make working alone hardest: task initiation, sustained attention, and self-monitoring.

What does body doubling actually look like?

It's quieter and weirder than you might expect.

In person: Two people sit in the same room. One is writing emails. The other is folding laundry.

They don't talk much. They might not talk at all. After 90 minutes, both are done with what they meant to do.

Virtually: Two people get on a video call. They greet each other, say what they're working on, then mute themselves and turn the camera at their screen or workspace. They work for an hour.

They check in for 30 seconds at the end. That's it.

In a co-working space or café: You're surrounded by other people working on their own things. There's no formal partnership, but the ambient presence of focused humans acts the same way. Many ADHD adults are vastly more productive in coffee shops than at home for exactly this reason.

The common thread: you're not alone, you're not being interrupted, and you're not being asked to perform. You're just present with another person who is also being present.

What if you don't have anyone to body double with?

Many people assume they need a friend who "gets it" to make this work. You don't.

The body doubler doesn't need to know about ADHD. They don't need to understand what they're doing.

What does the research say about body doubling?

Body doubling has almost no research history relative to how long ADHD adults have used it, but that has started changing fast. A wave of studies between 2023 and 2026 has begun measuring what was, until recently, purely anecdotal.

The largest survey to date. Eagle and colleagues' survey of 220 neurodivergent adults found that 186 of them (85%) reported being more likely to finish a task with someone else present, and task initiation was the second most common reason people used the strategy. Most had been body doubling for roughly four years before ever learning the term for it.

What brain activity shows (small, early). A 2025 EEG study by Schuenke and colleagues measured focus during reading tasks with and without a body double. Most metrics showed no statistically significant difference, but preference was clear: 71% of medicated ADHD participants, 56% of unmedicated ADHD participants, and 60% of a non-ADHD control group preferred working with a double present. Some unmedicated participants showed real gains, fewer distractions and faster completion, even when they didn't subjectively feel more focused.

What people actually reach for. A 2026 analysis by Yaukey and colleagues of over 3,000 comments across 114 r/ADHD threads about remote work found body doubling was the single most frequently cited technique for staying on track, with services like Focusmate coming up repeatedly.

It can wear off. Not every finding is positive. A 2026 study of ADHD adults who livestream their study sessions found the strategy can degrade over time: interruptions from viewers, performance anxiety, dependency on increasingly stimulating setups, and shame about being watched. Body doubling with strangers on camera is a different experience than a quiet co-working session.

It doesn't require another human. A small 2026 study had 11 university students with ADHD try a companion robot as a body double over two weeks. All but one kept using it voluntarily. Early evidence that presence itself, not necessarily a human one, is doing some of the work, which is consistent with why an AI companion can lower the activation barrier even without another person in the room.

The honest summary. The self-reported evidence for reducing task-initiation friction is consistent and reasonably large-scale (the 220-person survey). The objective, measured evidence (EEG, performance metrics) is early, small-sample, and mixed. Both can be true at once: people consistently say it helps them start, and the brain-level mechanism is still being worked out. The Russell Barkley executive function model, which frames ADHD as a self-regulation problem, gives a plausible reason why external presence would help, though the model itself isn't specific to body doubling.

None of this makes body doubling medication, and it isn't a replacement for one. But the direction of the research is toward "worth trying, matched to your own preferences" rather than "unproven."

How does body doubling fit with other ADHD tools?

Body doubling pairs well with almost every other ADHD strategy. A few combinations that work especially well:

Body doubling + the 2-minute rule. Use the 2-minute rule to start, the body double to keep going. The launch tool plus the sustain tool. Both are steps in the FOCO Start Method, which sequences them with three other moves.

Body doubling + Pomodoro. 25-minute focus blocks with a partner present. Take breaks together.

This is what most body-doubling apps default to.

Body doubling + a written task list. Tell your body double what you're working on at the start of the session. The act of saying it out loud creates a soft commitment your brain treats as accountability.

Body doubling + your best hours. Schedule sessions during the part of the day when your focus is naturally highest. You'll often get more done in a 90-minute session at your peak than in a full afternoon of scattered solo work.

FAQ

Is body doubling weird?

It feels weird the first time. By the second or third session, it feels obvious - like wondering why you ever tried to work alone. Many people who try it consistently describe it as the most underrated tool they have found.

Does the body double need to have ADHD?

No. They don't need to know anything about ADHD. They just need to be present and not interrupting. Their job is to exist, not to coach.

Can I body double with my partner or kids?

Yes. Anyone counts. The only requirement is that they're present and not pulling your attention away from your task. A spouse watching TV in the same room counts. A toddler does not.

How long should a session be?

Most apps default to 50 or 90 minutes. Many ADHD adults find 50 minutes is the sweet spot - long enough to actually finish something, short enough that it doesn't feel like a marathon.

Does it work for creative tasks?

Yes - sometimes better than for routine tasks. The friction of starting creative work is often the highest, and body doubling lowers that friction more than almost anything else.

What if I get distracted by the other person?

If you're chatting, you're not body doubling - you're hanging out. Real body doubling is mostly silent. If you and the partner can't keep silent, switch to virtual (mute the call) or find a different partner.

The Bottom Line

You don't have to do everything alone. In fact, your ADHD brain probably wasn't designed to.

The presence of another person - any person, in person or virtual, friend or stranger - seems to supply something you cannot reliably supply for yourself. That isn't weakness. It is worth using rather than arguing with.

Pick up your phone. Text someone. Get on a call. Start a FOCO session. Sit in a coffee shop. Just stop trying to white-knuckle this alone.

Your job is to get to the first 30 seconds. The rest takes care of itself.
