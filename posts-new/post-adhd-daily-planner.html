ADHD Daily Planner: A Free Printable Template That Fits One Page

The FOCO ADHD daily planner is a one page printable sheet with three priority slots, three rough time blocks, a brain dump area and a short habit strip. It is built for triage rather than planning, which is why it holds three things instead of twenty. Print it, fill in the top three, and if the day has already gone sideways, start a fresh sheet with today's date on it.

Key Takeaways

  • The daily sheet is for the days that already went wrong, not for careful morning planning. That is the whole reason it exists.
  • Three priority slots is a hard limit, not a suggestion. A page that holds twenty items gives you somewhere to hide from the three that matter.
  • Time blocks are labelled morning, afternoon and evening rather than clock times, because a schedule that assumes 9:15 punishes you at 9:20.
  • The brain dump box exists so working memory has somewhere to unload, which is what stops the mental loop of trying not to forget things.
  • Missed days leave no penalty display. There is no streak on the page to break, so nothing tells you that you have already failed.

What is on the ADHD daily planner?

One page, four blocks, and deliberately nothing else.

Top 3 Today holds the things that must happen. Three lines, three checkboxes, no room for a fourth.

Time Blocks splits the day into morning, afternoon and evening with three rules each. These are containers, not appointments.

Brain Dump is eight open lines for everything pulling at your attention that is not today's problem.

Today I... is a three row habit strip with blank labels, so you write the habits rather than inheriting someone else's list.

Across the top there is a date field, an energy check with three options, and one word for the day. The energy line matters more than it looks, because planning a low day like a high day is how the sheet becomes evidence against you.

When should you use a daily sheet instead of a weekly one?

Use the daily sheet on the days the week already fell apart.

That is the honest answer, and it is different from how most planners are sold. A weekly view is where you decide what matters. A daily view is where you salvage something after the week stopped following the plan.

The difference is the question each one asks. The weekly sheet asks what matters over the next seven days. The daily sheet asks one thing: what is the single next action.

If you are planning calmly on a Sunday, you want the ADHD weekly planner instead. If it is Wednesday at 2pm and you have lost the morning, this is the sheet.

Most people print more of these than they expect. Not every day needs one. The days that do, really do.

Why only three priorities?

Because the limit is what forces the decision.

A page with twenty lines lets you write twenty things and feel productive without choosing any of them. A page with three lines makes you rank, and ranking is the part that executive function struggles with most.

The National Institute of Mental Health describes ADHD as involving persistent difficulty with organising tasks and activities. Prioritising is a subset of that, and a list format quietly assumes you have already done it.

Three is also small enough to survive a bad day. Finishing three of three feels like a completed day. Finishing three of twenty feels like failure, even though it is the same amount of work.

If three still feels like too many, use the top line only and treat the other two as optional. Nobody is checking.

How do you fill in the time blocks without lying to yourself?

Write ranges, not clock times.

The sheet uses morning, afternoon and evening on purpose. A block that says 9:15 creates a fail condition at 9:20, and by 10am the whole page is void.

This is where time blindness does the damage. The gap between how long a task feels and how long it takes is not a discipline problem, it is a perception one, so a plan built on precise estimates is built on a measurement you cannot take.

Put one thing per block, two at most. If a block ends up empty, that is information about your actual capacity rather than a gap you failed to fill.

What is the brain dump box for?

Unloading working memory so it stops running in the background.

Holding six unrelated reminders in your head while trying to work is expensive, and working memory limits are one of the better documented parts of the ADHD profile. Writing things down is not organisation, it is offloading.

Use it for anything that surfaces and is not today's job. The email you remembered mid task. The thing you need to buy. The worry.

Nothing in that box needs to be actioned today. That is the point of keeping it separate from the top three.

What if you skip days?

Then you skip days, and the sheet does not record it.

There is no streak counter, no habit chain and no month view showing your gaps. This is deliberate, because a penalty display is the most reliable way to make someone stop opening a planner entirely.

The footer says it plainly: write today's date and start there. Yesterday's sheet is not a debt.

If the pattern is that you abandon every system after about two weeks, the sheet is not the problem and a different one will not fix it. That loop has its own causes, covered in why you keep abandoning ADHD planner apps.

Where the paper stops working

A sheet can hold your plan. It cannot start the task for you.

That gap is specific and it has a name. Task paralysis is the state where you know exactly what to do, the page says what to do, and the distance between knowing and moving stays fixed.

Paper has no answer for that moment because paper is passive. It waits.

FOCO breaks a task into one small step at a time and shows you only that step, and its AI focus companion stays present while you work rather than leaving you alone with the list. The printable and the app are not competing, they cover different halves of the same problem.

FAQ

Can I print the daily planner on A4 instead of Letter?

Yes. Set your printer to fit or scale to page and the layout holds, since the sheet uses proportional blocks rather than fixed millimetre positions.

Should I fill it in the night before or in the morning?

Night before, if you can. Deciding the top three when you are not yet under pressure removes one decision from a moment when decisions are hardest, and the morning version tends to become a to-do list instead of a priority list.

What is the difference between this and a regular daily to-do list?

The cap and the containers. A to-do list has no ceiling and no sense of when anything happens, so it grows until looking at it feels worse than ignoring it.

Do I need the weekly and monthly sheets too?

Not to start. Print a week of daily sheets first and see whether you reach for them, then add the other formats from the ADHD planner guide if you find you are missing a wider view.

The Bottom Line

The ADHD daily planner is one page built for the days that already went wrong. Three priorities, three rough blocks, somewhere to dump the noise, and no record of what you missed.

Print a small stack and leave them somewhere visible. A sheet in a drawer is a sheet you will not use.
