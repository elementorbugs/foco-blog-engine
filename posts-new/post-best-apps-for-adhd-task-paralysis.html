10 Best Apps for Beating ADHD Task Paralysis (2026)

The best apps for ADHD task paralysis work at the exact moment you're frozen, not just at the planning stage. FOCO breaks the stuck task into a first step small enough to actually start. Goblin Tools is the free alternative for the same job. Inflow adds CBT-based coaching for the pattern underneath the freeze. Calendar and to-do apps alone rarely help here - knowing what to do was never the problem.

Key Takeaways

  • Task paralysis is a starting problem, not a planning problem - apps that only organize tasks don't address it.
  • The apps that actually help share one trait: they shrink the first step until it's small enough to do without deciding anything.
  • Free options (Goblin Tools) work as well as paid ones for the breakdown step itself.
  • Body-doubling and AI companion features exist specifically for the moment willpower alone isn't enough.
  • An app that requires a long setup before it's useful adds a second task-initiation problem on top of the first one.

Why don't regular to-do list apps fix task paralysis?

A to-do list assumes the hard part is remembering what to do.

For task paralysis, that's not the hard part. You can have a perfectly organized list and still sit frozen in front of item one. The gap is between knowing and starting - task initiation deficit, not a memory or organization failure.

So the apps that actually help share a specific job: they shrink the task in front of you until starting takes no decision at all.

How did we evaluate these apps?

We looked at each app through one lens only: what happens in the first 60 seconds after you open it while frozen on a task.

Specifically: does it ask you to plan, or does it get you moving? How many taps between opening the app and doing something physical? Does it require setup before it's useful, or does it work cold? And does it cost anything to try the core feature, since paywall friction itself can be another freeze point.

We did not score apps on unrelated features (calendar sync, team collaboration) unless they directly affect the starting moment.

1. FOCO - best for the exact moment you're frozen

FOCO breaks one stuck task into small first steps and stays with you through the first one using an AI focus companion. It's built specifically for the freeze moment itself, not for planning the rest of your day around it.

Where it wins. Zero planning required - you type the task you're stuck on and get a tiny first step back immediately. The AI companion stays present while you work the first step, which matters most for the "I need someone here" version of the freeze.

Where it falls short. It's not a calendar or a project manager. If your problem is scheduling rather than starting, pair it with a separate planning app.

Pricing. Free tier covers the core breakdown feature. Paid tier adds unlimited sessions.

2. Goblin Tools - best free task breakdown

Goblin Tools is a free web tool, no signup required, that breaks any task into smaller concrete steps. If the barrier is purely "I don't know how to start this," Goblin Tools solves that specific problem at no cost.

Where it wins. Completely free, no account, no onboarding. Open the site, type the task, get steps back in seconds.

Where it falls short. No memory between sessions - the breakdown disappears when you close the tab. No presence or companion feature for the "I need someone with me" version of the freeze.

Pricing. Free on the web. A small one-time fee for the mobile app version.

3. Inflow - best for the pattern underneath the freeze

Inflow is a CBT-based coaching app with daily lessons and a moderated community, aimed at the thinking patterns that produce task paralysis repeatedly, not just today's stuck task.

Where it wins. Addresses why the freeze keeps happening, not just today's instance of it. The community adds accountability that a solo tool can't.

Where it falls short. Daily lessons take real time - it's not a quick fix for a task you're stuck on right now. Subscription-only, no meaningful free tier for the core content.

Pricing. Subscription required after a trial period; no free ongoing tier.

4. Finch - best low-stakes gamified starter

Finch pairs small self-care and task check-ins with a virtual pet that grows as you complete them. The stakes are deliberately low, which helps when the task itself feels too heavy to face directly.

Where it wins. Low pressure, gentle tone. Good for days when the freeze is tied to shame or overwhelm rather than simple forgetting.

Where it falls short. Not built for breaking down a specific complex task - it's better for small daily check-ins than for a genuinely large, stuck project.

Pricing. Free with optional paid subscription for extra features.

5. Habitica - best for turning routines into a game

Habitica turns recurring tasks into an RPG with experience points and rewards. Works best for repeating routines rather than one-off stuck tasks.

Where it wins. Genuinely fun for people who respond to game mechanics. Strong for building a routine over weeks, not just one frozen moment.

Where it falls short. The setup itself (customizing your avatar, party, habits) can become its own procrastination target. Not built for the "single task, right now" use case.

Pricing. Free with optional subscription for cosmetic and convenience features.

6. Sunsama - best for realistic daily planning

Sunsama forces a daily planning ritual that caps how much you commit to, which reduces the overwhelm-driven freeze that comes from an unrealistic backlog.

Where it wins. The daily planning ritual itself prevents the kind of backlog overwhelm that causes freezing in the first place. Clean calendar integration.

Where it falls short. No free tier. The daily planning ritual is itself a task some ADHD users skip, which defeats the purpose.

Pricing. Paid subscription only, with a free trial period.

7. Motion - best for auto-scheduling around a stuck task

Motion automatically re-slots tasks around your calendar using AI scheduling. Useful if the freeze is partly about not knowing when a task will fit, though it doesn't address the in-the-moment starting problem directly.

Where it wins. Removes the "when do I even do this" decision by auto-scheduling around your existing calendar.

Where it falls short. Doesn't break a task into smaller steps - it only decides when you'll attempt it. If the freeze is about starting, not scheduling, this alone won't fix it. Relatively expensive.

Pricing. Paid subscription only, no meaningful free tier.

8. Structured - best visual daily timeline

Structured lays the day out as a visual timeline instead of a list, which helps some ADHD brains see "now" more concretely than a checklist does.

Where it wins. The visual timeline makes "what should I be doing right now" obvious at a glance, which helps with time blindness alongside task paralysis.

Where it falls short. iOS only. Still fundamentally a planner, not a breakdown tool - it won't shrink an intimidating task into a smaller first step.

Pricing. Free tier available; paid tier unlocks templates and integrations.

9. Tiimo - best for combining visual planning with routines

Tiimo is a visual planner built for ADHD and autism, using timelines and icons instead of a plain list. It's closer to a planning tool than a starting tool, so pair it with a breakdown app for the freeze moment itself.

Where it wins. Built specifically for neurodivergent users, with visual and icon-based cues that reduce reliance on reading a list. Strong for building repeatable routines.

Where it falls short. Like Structured, it plans and displays tasks - it doesn't break a specific stuck task into smaller steps.

Pricing. Free tier with limited features; paid subscription unlocks the full planner.

10. Todoist - honest note on why plain to-do apps fall short here

Todoist is a clean, capable to-do app for people without task initiation deficit. For ADHD task paralysis specifically, a list by itself rarely closes the gap between knowing and starting - it's worth knowing that going in, rather than blaming yourself when a clean list app doesn't fix the freeze.

Where it wins. Excellent organization, reminders, and cross-device sync for people whose main issue is remembering tasks, not starting them.

Where it falls short. No breakdown feature, no companion, no mechanism aimed at the starting moment itself. A long list can itself increase the freeze by making the backlog visible all at once.

Pricing. Free tier available; paid tier adds reminders, labels, and automation.

FAQ: Apps for ADHD Task Paralysis

What's the single best app to try first for task paralysis?

Start with a free task-breakdown tool like Goblin Tools or FOCO's free tier. If the freeze keeps recurring across many different tasks, Inflow's coaching approach addresses the underlying pattern rather than one task at a time.

Do gamified apps like Habitica actually help with task paralysis?

They help most with recurring routines rather than one-off stuck tasks, since the reward mechanic needs repetition to build momentum. For a single frozen task right now, a breakdown tool tends to work faster.

Why doesn't my regular to-do app fix this?

Most to-do apps assume the hard part is remembering the task. In task paralysis, you already know the task - the gap is between knowing and starting, which a plain list doesn't address.

Can body doubling apps replace a real accountability partner?

Not exactly the same experience, but many people find an AI or virtual presence enough to break the freeze, especially when a real person isn't available in the moment.

Are free apps as good as paid ones for breaking task paralysis specifically?

For the core breakdown feature, yes - Goblin Tools is free and does the job. Paid apps tend to add extras (coaching content, calendar sync, companion presence) rather than a fundamentally better breakdown.

What if I've tried several apps and none of them stick?

That's common, and it usually means the app doesn't match your specific freeze point. Use the comparison table above to match the tool to what's actually stuck - starting, scheduling, or motivation - rather than switching to another general productivity app.

Can I use more than one of these apps together?

Yes, and many people do - a breakdown tool for the starting moment plus a visual planner like Tiimo or Structured for the rest of the day covers two different problems that a single app rarely solves alone.
