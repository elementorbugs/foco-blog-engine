Joon App Review: The ADHD Chore Game for Kids

Short answer: Joon is a chore app for kids ages 6 to 12 with ADHD: parents assign tasks, and approved tasks earn coins for a virtual pet game. It suits families who will stay involved, with a free version and premium at $12.99 a month or from $89.99 a year.

  • Try Joon if your child is 6 to 12, daily routines turn into arguments, and a game reward actually motivates them.
  • Skip it if you are trying to cut screen time, your child is older than 12, or you cannot keep reviewing tasks each day.
  • Pick FOCO, the app we make, if the person who cannot get started is you, because it splits your own stuck task into timed steps.

What does Joon look like in use?

We did not test Joon hands-on. The screens below are from its official App Store images, so they show the app as the developer presents it.

The parent side is a task manager. You pick quests from templates or write your own, set them into a daily agenda, then approve or reject what your child submits, sometimes with a photo attached.

The child side is a game. Coins from approved quests go toward feeding and caring for a virtual pet called a Doter, and toward exploring the game world.

Is Joon good for kids with ADHD?

It can be a useful motivation tool, but it is not a treatment, and Joon's own blog says it is not meant as a stand-alone therapeutic intervention. The evidence so far comes mostly from the company itself.

The idea has a sensible base. The CDC notes that for children 6 and older, recommendations include medication and behavior therapy together, with parent training in behavior management for kids up to age 12.

CHADD also describes behavioral techniques as a key part of treatment for children with ADHD. Joon borrows the clear-task, quick-reward pattern, which fits how we understand ADHD and motivation, but an app does not replace that training.

What does Joon's research show?

Joon's website reports an observational study of children ages 6 to 12 after eight weeks of use. Its research page links a white paper, which reports nearly 30% fewer disruptive behaviors by parent report, and 73% of parents agreeing it reduced arguments.

Read those as promising, not proven. The paper was prepared by Joon's own staff with 90 enrolled parents and no comparison group, and a listed randomized controlled trial had no results posted when we checked.

Other company numbers need care too. The App Store says over two hundred thousand families and the homepage says 1 million+, and we could not confirm either, or the claim that 90% of kids complete all assigned tasks.

What does Joon get right?

It moves the reminding: The pet and the app handle prompts, which may take some heat out of the morning. Many parents of kids with ADHD will recognize how quickly reminders turn into conflict.

Parents stay in the loop: By default a task pays out after you approve it, and the App Store images show bonus rewards for a job well done. Release notes also mention auto-verification for photo quests and AI review of submissions, if checking every one is too much.

Low effort to start: Templates cover basics like brushing teeth and making the bed. Separate devices are optional, though Joon says it works best when a child has their own.

Where might Joon fall short?

The interest may fade: ChoosingTherapy rates Joon 4.5 out of 5, yet sums up Reddit reviews as finding it "overpriced and only good for a short period." Timily, which sells a screen time app, and NeuroLaunch, which cites Timily elsewhere, say engagement drops after 4 to 8 weeks, without naming a source.

The reward is more screen: The motivation is a video game, and the app-lock feature trades coins for time on apps like YouTube. That is a fair deal for some families and the wrong one for others.

It runs on you: Someone has to write quests, review them and add new ones, and Joon's listing says to stay involved. Dinkum Tribe, a parent blogger who used it with four kids, says setup took a couple of hours.

A narrow age window: ChoosingTherapy lists it as not optimized for teens, who may find it too childish. If your child is already 11 or 12, think twice before buying a full year.

Setup details vary by source: ChoosingTherapy describes a separate Joon Pet Game for the child, and the stores list one, while Joon's listing talks about one app on a shared or separate device. Older sources also say parents need an iPhone, though the current Google Play listing describes family setup on Android.

How much does Joon cost?

Joon has a free version, a 7-day premium trial, and premium at $12.99 a month or an annual plan "starting at $89.99," according to its site. The App Store lists several "All Access" prices from $12.99 to $149.99 without saying which plan each one is.

Check the paywall on your own device before you commit. ChoosingTherapy says reduced rates are available for families in need if you contact Joon directly, and some App Store reviewers it cites say they were charged after canceling a trial, so note your trial end date.

Joon's terms say refunds are at the company's discretion, with requests due within a typical 30-day window. ChoosingTherapy adds that App Store buyers must go through Apple for a refund.

What does Joon do with kids' data?

Joon's privacy policy says it obtains verifiable parental consent before collecting personal information from children, including photos. It also requires that consent before turning on AI features children can access for users under 13.

Quests can involve children uploading photos, and the policy says both that certain authorized personnel may decrypt and view them and that they are accessible to a single team member. The App Store's 13+ rating page also lists messaging and chat plus user-generated content, so read the policy and look through the app together.

Is Joon different from FOCO?

Yes, because they serve different people. Joon is for a parent assigning tasks to a child, while FOCO, the app we make, is for the person who is stuck on their own task.

That stuck moment is task initiation, the step between knowing what to do and doing it (our guide to task initiation deficit explains it). In FOCO, AI splits the task into small steps, each with a time estimate, and a focus timer runs with an AI body-doubling companion on screen.

Who should try Joon?

  • Routines like teeth, backpack and bedtime cause the same fight every day.
  • You can check and approve quests on most days.
  • You are comfortable with game time as the reward.

FAQ

Is the Joon app free?

Joon has a free version that its site says families can use indefinitely, with fewer features. Premium costs $12.99 a month or from $89.99 a year, after a 7-day trial.

What age is Joon for?

Joon says its game is for ages 6 to 12. ChoosingTherapy notes that it is not optimized for teens.

Is Joon safe for kids?

Joon's privacy policy says it requires verifiable parental consent before collecting children's personal information, including photos. The App Store rates it 13+ and lists messaging and chat plus user-generated content, so parents should look through it first.

Does Joon work for ADHD?

Joon reports parent-rated improvements in its own observational study, but results from its controlled trial were not posted when we checked. It is not a treatment, so treat it as a motivation tool alongside any care your child already gets.

Does Joon limit screen time?

Joon's App Store images and release notes show parents locking apps like Roblox until quests are done and approving unlock requests. NeuroLaunch and Timily say it has no screen time management, so check that the feature works on your plan and device.

What is a Doter in Joon?

A Doter is the virtual pet your child feeds, washes and grows in the game. Your child pays for that care with coins earned from approved quests.
