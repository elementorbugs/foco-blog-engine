Why do people procrastinate?

Mostly to escape a bad feeling, not out of laziness. Researchers Fuschia Sirois and Timothy Pychyl argue procrastination "has a great deal to do with short-term mood repair and emotion regulation," in a 2013 paper.

The task feels boring, scary or too big, so you do something that feels better right now. Your phone is the easiest thing that feels better.

It is also common. About 15 to 20 percent of the general population are procrastinators, according to a summary of Piers Steel's 2007 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin.

What actually helps you stop procrastinating?

Research points to a few methods that work, and each app on this list uses one of them. Pick the method first.

Put something at stakeA real cost for skipping makes the future consequence feel closer.Beeminder, Forest

Tiny, specific first steps: A meta-analysis by Gollwitzer and Sheeran found that if-then plans had an effect of "medium-to-large magnitude (d= .65) on goal attainment," across 94 tests. "At 9 am I open the doc and write one sentence" beats "work on the report."

Friction: one sec says a study run with researchers and published in PNAS in 2023 found its pause reduced app use by 57%. The study was co-authored by one sec's founder, so treat it as promising rather than independent.

Company: In a survey of 220 neurodivergent adults by Eagle and colleagues, 85% said they were more likely to finish a task with someone else there, a method called body doubling.

What are the best procrastination apps?

These are the anti-procrastination apps we recommend, grouped by the reason they fix. We did not test them hands-on; every fact comes from official listings and our own reviews.

1. FOCO: best when you know the task but cannot start

FOCO, the app we make, is built for the moment you know what to do and still cannot begin. AI splits the task into small steps, each with a time estimate, and a focus timer runs with a body-doubling companion on screen.

It costs $59 a year with a 7-day free trial, or $9 a month billed right away, on iPhone and Android. We also run a free focus room with no camera, account or booking.

2. Goblin Tools: best when the task feels too big

Goblin Tools' Magic ToDo turns one vague task into a checklist of steps in seconds. The website is free with no account, and the phone apps are a one-time $1.99 on the US App Store.

It gives you the steps but has no timer or reminders. See our FOCO vs Goblin Tools comparison.

3. Forest: best when you keep picking up your phone

Forest grows a virtual tree while you stay off your phone, and the tree withers if you leave early. It is free on iPhone and Android, with an optional Forest Plus subscription.

It helps you stay with a task once you start, but not start one. See our Forest review.

4. one sec: best when you open apps on autopilot

one sec adds a short pause, like a deep breath or a question about why you are opening the app, before Instagram or TikTok loads. Its essentials are free, and it runs on iPhone, Android, Mac and in browsers.

It has 4.8 stars from 23,856 US App Store ratings. It is gentler than a full block, which suits people who still need those apps for work.

5. Opal: best for blocking apps on a schedule

Opal blocks the apps and sites you pick on a schedule, with a focus score and gems as rewards. The free plan gives you one rule, and Pro is $99.99 a year, $19.99 a month or $399 once.

Its Pro Hard Mode stops you from cancelling a block. See our Opal review.

6. Brick: best when you switch off blockers in seconds

Brick is a $59 magnetic puck that locks the apps you choose until you tap your phone on it again, with no subscription. It works because you have to walk to the Brick to undo it.

It has 4.9 stars from about 58,700 US App Store ratings, but it only blocks your phone. See our Brick review.

7. Freedom: best for blocking every device at once

Freedom blocks apps, websites or the whole internet across your phone, tablet and computer in one session. It runs on Mac, Windows, iPhone, Android, Chromebook and Linux.

There is a free plan, and Premium is $8.99 a month, or $3.33 a month billed yearly with a 7-day free trial. A lifetime plan lists at $199, and it was on sale for $99.50 when we checked on November 14, 2026.

8. Cold Turkey Blocker: best for a computer block you cannot undo

Cold Turkey blocks websites, apps or your whole computer on Windows and Mac, and its locks are built to be very hard to undo. The free version blocks websites, and Pro is a one-time US$45.

There is no phone version. See our Cold Turkey Blocker review.

9. Focusmate: best when you need a person

Focusmate pairs you with another person on a 25, 50 or 75 minute video call, and you both work. It is free for 3 sessions a week, then $8 a month billed yearly, and your camera has to be on.

See our Focusmate review, or our Flow Club review for hosted groups.

10. Structured: best when your day has no plan

Structured lays your day out as a timeline of time blocks, and most of it is free. It had 4.8 stars from over 168,000 US App Store ratings when we checked.

A task with a time slot is easier to start than one floating on a list. See our Structured review.

11. Beeminder: best when nothing is at stake

Beeminder tracks a goal against a red line on a graph, and you pay a pledge you set if you fall off track. The core plan is free, so you only pay when you slip, with optional premium plans from $8 a month.

It suits people who respond to real stakes, and it can backfire if losing money adds shame. Its iPhone app is a companion for entering data, with most of the work on the website.

12. Brainway: best for spotting your patterns

Brainway is a procrastination app where you log mood, sleep, habits and focus each day, and it charts which ones line up with good and bad days. You need a subscription to keep using it.

Most reviews on its US App Store page were about billing when we checked, so note the renewal date. See our Brainway review.

What about quiz-first apps like Liven, Wisey and Chillio?

You may see ads for apps that start with a quiz about procrastination or ADHD, then sell a subscription. They are real apps, but read the checkout screen carefully.

  • Liven is an AI life coach for stress and emotions, not a procrastination tool, and its web plans renew separately from the App Store. See our Liven review.
  • Wisey sells short courses plus a timer and blocker, and most complaints are about a cheap first period that renews at about $48.50 every 28 days. See our Wisey review.
  • Chillio is a cute habit and cleaning planner, and unhappy reviewers say the web sign-up charged them for extras they did not mean to buy. See our Chillio review.

None of their quizzes is a diagnosis, and none of these apps is built to help you start a specific task.

How do you make a procrastination app actually work?

An app only helps if you set it up for your reason. These steps take about 15 minutes.

  1. Name your one reason: Pick the single answer from the cards at the top that fits most days.
  2. Set it up while calm: Choose blocks, schedules and pledges when you are not already stuck, so the decision is made before the urge hits.
  3. Shrink the first step: Write it as one physical action under 5 minutes, like "open the file," not "do the report."
  4. Give it a time: Put that first step in your calendar as an if-then plan, such as "after lunch, I open the file."
  5. Add a person if alone fails: A body-doubling session or a friend on a call can carry you past the start.
  6. Review after a week: If the app adds guilt instead of getting you started, switch to one that fixes a different reason.

Are procrastination apps good for ADHD?

They can help, if they match the problem. With ADHD, putting things off is often not choosing to wait but being unable to start, which is linked to differences in executive function, as Russell Barkley's fact sheet explains.

Our guide to procrastination vs. paralysis explains the difference. If freezing is your problem, our list of apps for ADHD task paralysis goes deeper.

For ADHD itself, the National Institute of Mental Health is a better place to start.

FAQ

What is the best app for procrastination?

It depends on why you procrastinate. FOCO, which we make, and Goblin Tools help you start; Forest, one sec, Opal and Brick fight phone distraction; Freedom and Cold Turkey block computers; Focusmate adds a person; and Beeminder adds stakes.

Is there a free procrastination app?

Yes. Goblin Tools' website, Forest and Structured are free, and one sec, Opal, Freedom, Cold Turkey, Focusmate and Beeminder all have free plans.

Do anti-procrastination apps work?

They work best when they fix your specific reason. Research supports tiny if-then first steps, friction before distractions and working alongside someone, and each app here uses at least one of those.

What is the best app blocker for procrastination?

For your phone, Opal for schedules, one sec for gentle pauses or Brick for a physical lock. For computers, Cold Turkey for strict locks or Freedom to block every device at once.

Which procrastination app is best for ADHD?

If you freeze before you begin, pick an app that breaks the task into small steps, like FOCO or Goblin Tools. If you get pulled away once you start, a blocker like Opal, Freedom or Forest may help more.

Is Liven a procrastination app?

Not really. Liven is an AI life coach for stress and emotions, and it has no task breakdown or calendar, according to our review.

Are procrastination apps worth paying for?

Try the free option first. Most apps here have a free plan or trial, so you can check whether the app fixes your reason before you pay.

How do I stop procrastinating without an app?

Write the first step as one tiny action, tie it to a time with an if-then plan, put your phone in another room, and work next to someone. Apps only make these methods easier to keep up.
