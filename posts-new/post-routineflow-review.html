Short answer: RoutineFlow is a routine app for ADHD that turns a routine into a chain of timed steps, so you only face one small step at a time. It is best for repeating sequences like mornings and bedtime, it has a free version with Pro on top, and users praise its simplicity while some report freezes and lost reminders.

  • Try RoutineFlow if your mornings or evenings fall apart between steps and a timer per step keeps you moving.
  • Skip it if you need web or Apple Watch access, or most of your stuck tasks change every day.
  • Pick FOCO if the hard task is new each time and you want it split into timed steps, with a focus companion on screen.

What does RoutineFlow look like in use?

We did not test RoutineFlow hands-on. The screens below are from its official App Store images, and the details come from its listings and user reviews.

You build a routine as a list of steps, each with an emoji and a duration. Then you press play, and the app shows one step with a countdown until you mark it done.

Is RoutineFlow built for ADHD?

Yes. Its listing says it was "designed with ADHD users in mind" and leans on structure, timers and small steps rather than long lists.

That fits a real ADHD pain point. Routines lean on executive function, the brain skills behind sequencing and keeping track of steps (our executive function guide explains more), and a guided sequence takes some of that load off.

What does RoutineFlow get right for ADHD brains?

One step on screen: Instead of a list you have to hold in your head, you see the current step and a countdown. Our guide to ADHD morning routines explains why that matters most in the first hour of the day.

Time made visible: Each step has a duration, and the app shows roughly when the routine will finish. That helps with time blindness, trouble sensing how long things take (our time blindness guide goes deeper).

AI subtasks: If one step feels too big, the app can split it into smaller ones, according to its App Store screenshots.

A lifetime option: Several App Store reviewers thank the developer for a one-time purchase, because they cannot afford many app subscriptions.

Where does RoutineFlow fall short?

Reliability complaints: Some recent App Store reviews report the app freezing when ending a routine, reminder times disappearing and a halfway alert that stopped working after an update.

Unclear free version: Neither the listing nor the official site says what the free version includes, so you only find the limits inside the app.

Fewer platforms: It is a phone app for iPhone and Android, with no web version or Apple Watch app listed.

Updates have slowed: The current iOS version dates from March 2026, and the official website still describes version 1.0.

How much does RoutineFlow cost?

It is free to download, with Pro as an in-app purchase. On the US App Store, Pro is listed as weekly, monthly, yearly and lifetime options, from $5.99 a week to $99.99 for lifetime access, and the price you see can depend on the offer shown.

Google Play lists a free trial and in-app purchases. Check the paywall in your own region before you pay, and try the free version first.

Is RoutineFlow worth it?

For repeating routines, it can be. Many users rate it highly, and the lifetime option makes the cost predictable if it sticks.

Given the reliability complaints, use the free version for a week of real mornings first. If it survives a bad day, Pro is easier to justify.

How is RoutineFlow different from Routinery?

They work the same way: a routine as a chain of timed steps. Routinery is bigger, with 18,076 US App Store ratings, voice guidance, and Apple Watch and Mac apps, plus a free plan of 2 routines (our Routinery review).

RoutineFlow has AI subtasks and a lifetime option, but fewer platforms. If you want voice prompts or a watch, lean Routinery; if you want a single purchase, lean RoutineFlow.

How is RoutineFlow different from FOCO?

RoutineFlow is built for routines you repeat. FOCO, the app we make, is built for the one-off task you keep putting off.

That gap is task initiation, the step between knowing what to do and doing it (our guide to task initiation deficit explains it). In FOCO you name the task, AI splits it into small timed steps, and step one opens into a focus session with a companion on screen.
